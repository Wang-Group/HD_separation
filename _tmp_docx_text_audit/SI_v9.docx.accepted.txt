[0062] style=Heading2 table=False Overview
[0063] style= table=False The electrochemical platform is designed for the autonomous optimization of catalysts towards hydrogen isotope separation during the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) (Figure S1A). The platform consists of six modules for catalyst synthesis through electrodeposition, as well as its performance evaluation. These modules include the electrode storage module, the material transfer module, the electrodeposition module for the synthesis of catalysts, the catalyst post-treatment module, the electrochemical testing module, and the mass spectrometry testing module. Altogether, these modules form an integrated electrochemical system for catalyst preparation, post-treatment, and characterization.
[0064] style=Caption table=False Figure S1. Electrochemical automation platform. (A) The overall setup of the robotic platform for electrocatalysis. The platform includes (a) the material transport module with four-axis robotic arm (MG400), (b) a working electrode storage module, (c) a rotating module to change the orientation of the working electrodes, (d) an electrodeposition module containing four cells for parallel experimentation, (e) catalyst post-treatment module with drying unit to store synthesized catalysts, (f) electrochemical testing module using a H-type electrolytic cell, (g) a series of syringe pumps, switching valves, and peristaltic pumps for liquid handling, (h) stock solutions of metal precursor solution, sodium citrate solution, etc., (i) a catalyst post-treatment module to wash the electrodes, (j) a mass spectrometry testing module, (k) an eight-channel potentiostat, and (l) an in-line mass spectrometer for gas detection. (B) Workflow of the electrochemical automation platform.
[0065] style= table=False The workflow of the platform is outlined as follows (Figure S1B): Upon receiving experimental instructions from the host computer, all instruments are initialized, and the response of H2 and HD of the mass spectrometry is calibrated. The four-axis robotic arm then takes the working electrode from the electrode storage module and places it into the 3D printed single-chamber cells for electrodeposition. A syringe pump and switching valve are used to extract and inject the solution into the electrodeposition cells. Four electrodeposition cells are fabricated for parallel experimentation and improved efficiency. Electrodeposition was performed using four channels of a potentiostat. After preparation of the catalysts, the peristaltic pumps, syringe pump, and switching valve are used to clean the electrode and the cells. Both sides of the electrode sheets were rinsed five times with deionized water using a syringe pump. The electrolytic cell was subjected to a single acid-washing procedure (acid-washing solution prepared from 400 mL water, 100 mL concentrated hydrochloric acid, and 30 mL nitric acid), followed by soaking and five subsequent rinses with deionized water. The cleaned electrode is then transferred by the robotic arm to the drying area, and the dried catalyst is moved into the H-type electrolytic cell. Finally, the electrochemical performance and H/D separation coefficients of the catalysts are evaluated using the potentiostat and the in-line gas analysis mass spectrometer. The data was processed and used to establish the machine learning model, and to generate new experiments, closing the loop for the discovery of catalysts.

Since mass spectrometric analysis requires a constant injection flow and pressure, as well as appropriate system humidity, the N₂ carrier gas flow was precisely controlled at 20 mL/min using a mass flow controller, while a high-precision syringe pump was employed for sample injection, thereby ensuring reliable data acquisition. In addition, the calibration curve of the mass spectrometer was remeasured after each instrument restart. In the area of Figure S2A(a), three gas bags are manually prepared, filled with pure N2, 0.5% HD (HD purity 96%), and 12% H2 (H2 purity 99.999%), respectively. The prepared gas bags are connected to separate syringe pumps (3 mL) as shown in Figure S2, and the connections are sealed with sealing film to prevent gas leakage. During the mass spectrometer calibration, the total volume extracted by the HD and H2 pumps is controlled to 4 mL, while maintaining the total flow rate at 4/120 mL/s. The linear response curves between the mass spectrometer peak intensities of H₂ and HD and their respective volumes were obtained by varying the HD/H2 ratio. Over the test duration of more than 24 hours, the results from three parallel experiments remained linear, with an R² value greater than 0.99, fulfilling the experimental requirements. One example from this calibration is shown in Figure S2B.

[0079] style= table=False The electrodeposition module was initially designed with a single-chamber cell for electrodeposition to prepare catalysts, and later extended to parallel experimentation to four cells (Figure S4). To resolve this problem, a structure shown in Figure S4(A)(b) was designed and implemented, whose core is a printed circuit board (PCB). The central via of the PCB is connected by soldering to a gold-plated retractable spring contact probe (33.35mm). Printed wiring connects the central via to another via, which is soldered to a crocodile clip test lead. The crocodile clip then connects to the potentiostat. During electrochemical testing, the IO system controls the pneumatic cylinder to move the spring probe forward, ensuring contact with the conductive area on the back of the working electrode plate (Figure S3(A)) and completing the circuit. To ensure repeatability and accuracy in the linear motion of the probe, springs with appropriate stiffness and rod screws are installed at the two diagonals of the PCB. Additionally, in this module, the pneumatic cylinder connected to an aluminum profile of suitable length controls two cells simultaneously. To ensure the linear motion of the cylinder and aluminum profile, linear guide rails are installed on both sides of the aluminum profile, as shown in Figure S4(A)(b).

[0101] style= table=False Catalysts were subjected to electrolysis at a constant current of –50 mA cm–2 for 1600 s. After 5 min of electrolysis, gas from the headspace was extracted at a rate of (14/3)/120 mL/s and injected into the mass spectrometer at the same rate. Once the mass spectrometry signal stabilized, a second injection was performed. Each catalyst was tested three times, and the separation factor was determined by averaging the last two results. The separation factor (S) is a standard measure of the efficiency of hydrogen/deuterium separation technologies. It reflects the relative enrichment of isotopes H and D in the gas phase compared to the liquid phase during electrolysis9. Based on Faraday’s law, , under the given electrolysis conditions, the consumption of H2O and D2O is negligible compared to the initial electrolyte. During the electrolysis process, the amount of D2 produced is extremely small, almost indistinguishable from the baseline of the mass spectrometry. Therefore, the produced D2 can be neglected in the calculation of the separation factor. The separation factor was calculated as follows Eq. 1.

[0103] style=Heading3 table=False Linear sweep voltammetry measurement
[0104] style= table=False After a constant current test for 1600 s, no other pre-activation treatment was performed on the catalyst prior to the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) test. Before the LSV test, the cell resistances were determined using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Specifically, an alternating current (AC) signal of 10 kHz and 50 mV was applied at open circuit potential (OCP), and impedance was measured once per second for a total of five measurements. The average value of these five measurements was taken as the cell resistance (R). In the LSV testing process, 90% iR (current*resistance) compensation was applied. The LSV test was conducted within a potential range from 0.0068 V to -0.373 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) at a scan rate of 2 mV/s, ensuring that the electrode surface remained as close as possible to a steady-state polarization condition. Based on the obtained LSV curves, the overpotential at a given current density was calculated using Eq. 2 to Eq. 4, serving as a criterion for evaluating the catalytic activity. To calculate the Tafel slope, the current density range from –2 to –40 mA cm-2 was selected. For each integer current density within this range, a linear fit of log|j| versus overpotential was performed within a ±5 mV potential window. This procedure yielded a local Tafel slope corresponding to each current density point. The resulting Tafel slope values were then plotted as a function of current density. The final Tafel slope was defined as the converged value, where the slope stabilized without further significant variation. All data were batch-processed using a custom program, and the corresponding code is available at https://github.com/Wang-Group/HD_separation.

[0117] style=Heading1 table=False Interpretable Machine Learning on Bimetallic Catalysts
[0118] style=Heading2 table=False Overview
[0119] style= table=False To identify the physicochemical factors governing H/D separation across the initially screened bimetallic catalysts, we performed an interpretable machine-learning analysis using the complete primary screening dataset after preprocessing and quality filtering. [0193] style=Heading1 table=False Multi-objective Closed-loop Optimization of Bimetallic Catalysts
[0194] style=Heading2 table=False Overview
[0195] style= table=False With the data from the initial screening of bimetallic catalysts with a fixed ratio among metals and additives, Bayesian optimization was further implemented to optimize the recipes for various combinations of the two metals. The search space was defined by the concentrations of the species in the solution for electrodeposition. The process was formulated as a bi-objective optimization problem: one is the H-D separation coefficient, and the other is the capability of hydrogen evolution reaction of the catalysts, which is characterized by its overpotential at –10 mA cm–2. A Gaussian process was selected as the surrogate model, and the expected hypervolume improvement was utilized to design a batch of four new experiments (three new reactions with one reference to validate the stability of the system) in line. The robotic platform detected the new experiments and performed the corresponding synthesis and characterization of the catalysts. This process iterated until the end of the optimization.

Overall, the Tafel-shift analysis suggests an isotope sensitivity trend of CoV > CoMo > Co > Ni, which is in better agreement with the experimentally observed H/D separation performance.

In the second stage, the volume decreased from 75 to 7.5 mL, followed by the same post-treatment procedure. In the third stage, the volume decreased from 100 to 10 mL. For the fourth to sixth stages, the volume decreased from 80 to 8 mL in each stage, with identical post-treatment steps applied.
[0315] style= table=False The deuterium content in the collected water after each stage was quantified by quantitative 2H NMR spectroscopy using an internal standard method. The distilled samples were mixed with a known amount of DMSO-d6 as the internal standard. Spectra were recorded under quantitative conditions with a sufficiently long relaxation delay to ensure accurate integration. The deuterium content was determined by comparing the integrated 2H signal of water with that of the internal standard. The D concentration increased progressively from an initial value of 0.0156% to 0.09%, 0.45%, 2.33%, 12.72%, 44.95%, and finally to 98.75% after six enrichment stages, demonstrating the effectiveness of the FeNi catalyst and the multistage enrichment strategy.
