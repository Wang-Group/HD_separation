[8] Abstract

[9] Deuterium is essential for pharmaceuticals, nuclear fusion, and advanced manufacturing, yet its natural abundance in water is only ~0.0156%, making large-scale enrichment inherently energy intensive. Meanwhile, industrial water electrolysis for green hydrogen is becoming competitive, creating a timely opportunity to couple hydrogen production with isotope separation. Here, we report an autonomous electrochemical discovery platform that simultaneously targets deuterium enrichment and hydrogen evolution. The system automates electrodeposition to prepare catalysts, electrochemical testing, and online mass-spectrometric isotope analysis with real-time data processing, enabling rapid and reproducible quantification of both the H/D separation coefficient and hydrogen-evolution activity under alkaline conditions. Using this platform, we obtained a dataset comprising ~700 catalysts and ~4200 electrochemical measurements. Interpretable machine-learning analysis reveals that isotope separation is governed by element-specific electronegativity, H adsorption energetics, and work-function-related electronic descriptors. Guided by these descriptors, Bayesian optimization explores the composition space of four promising bimetallic systems (CoMo, CoV, CoNi, and FeNi). The optimized catalysts exhibit pronounced expansion of the activity-selectivity performance envelope, and their distribution in descriptor space follows physically meaningful trends rather than black-box search. Long-term galvanostatic electrolysis with the stable FeNi catalyst enriches deuterium in water from natural abundance to 98.75% in six stages. Together, this work establishes an autonomous electrochemical discovery framework for coupling sustainable hydrogen production with efficient deuterium enrichment.

[10] Introduction

[11] Deuterium (D), a stable hydrogen isotope, is increasingly demanded for deuterated drugs1, isotope tracing2, semiconductors3, optical fibers4, nuclear fusion research5, and other applications6,7. Despite the abundance of hydrogen in water, deuterium constitutes only ~0.0156% of natural hydrogen, making its large-scale enrichment intrinsically energy-intensive and economically challenging.

[12] Conventional enrichment technologies such as distillation8, cryogenic rectification9, and chemical exchange10 exploit subtle isotope-dependent differences in physical and chemical properties. Because the differences are intrinsically small, efficient separation typically requires multistage cascades, leading to high energy consumption. Gas diffusion and chromatographic methods can enhance separation efficiency but remain economically challenging for large-scale production following the global demand11-13.

[13] Electrolysis-based isotope separation14-33 offers an alternative, taking advantage of differences in zero-point vibrational energies (ZPEH–O = 5.0 kcal mol–1, ZPED–O = 3.7 kcal mol–1) and quantum tunneling rates between H and D34,35, enabling kinetic discrimination during hydrogen evolution. This technique has long been considered but remained undeveloped for large-scale production due to high energy demand.

[14] However, today, rapid advances in water electrolysis driven by the global energy transition have made large-scale green hydrogen production technically mature and increasingly cost-competitive. Progress in electrolyzer design, renewable electricity integration, and system engineering has enabled sustained operation at industrial current densities, positioning electrolysis as a cornerstone technology for a low-carbon energy infrastructure36-39. These developments brought a timely opportunity to integrate hydrogen generation with isotope separation, obtaining enriched deuterium as a by-product of green hydrogen generation, potentially significantly reducing the cost for deuterium production.

[15] Catalysts are crucial to enable such coupled electrochemical systems. Ideally, electrocatalysts should combine high hydrogen evolution activity with strong H/D isotope selectivity. However, progress to date remains limited. Only a small number of materials have been reported to exhibit measurable isotope separation during electrolysis, and most suffer from a pronounced trade-off between catalytic activity and selectivity23,26-32. Moreover, the physicochemical origins of isotope discrimination at electrochemical interfaces are still poorly understood, and generalizable design principles are lacking. These gaps severely constrain rational catalyst development and underscore the demand for systematic strategies that can simultaneously optimize performance and uncover mechanistic insight.

[16] Recent advances in laboratory automation and artificial intelligence offer a powerful route to overcome these limitations. Closed-loop discovery frameworks that integrate robotic experimentation with data-driven modeling have begun to accelerate catalyst development and uncover hidden structure–property relations40-44. By intelligently designing experiments, acquiring data, and updating predictive models autonomously, such systems enable efficient navigation of complex chemical spaces that are inaccessible to manual screening. These strategies are suitable for hydrogen isotope separation to resolve the fundamental trade-off between activity and selectivity.

[17] Here, we develop an autonomous electrochemical discovery platform to explore hydrogen isotope separation during water electrolysis (Figure 1A). The system integrates robotic catalyst synthesis, automated electrochemical testing, and online isotope analysis, enabling rapid, reproducible quantification of both H/D separation efficiency and hydrogen evolution activity under alkaline conditions. Using this platform, we construct a large experimental dataset comprising around 700 catalysts and 4200 electrochemical measurements. Besides electrolysis activity and H/D separation performance, electrochemical characterizations of these catalysts, including linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), cyclic voltammetry (CV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), are also systematically recorded. Statistical analysis of EIS spectra and data-driven analysis reveals key physicochemical factors governing isotope discrimination, including electronegativity, H adsorption energetics, and work-function-related electronic descriptors, providing general design insights. Guided by these principles, closed-loop Bayesian optimization efficiently navigates catalyst composition space and systematically pushes the activity-selectivity performance frontier toward higher separation efficiency and improved catalytic activity (Figure 1B).

[20] 1. Closed-loop Discovery of Electrocatalysts for H/D Separation in Alkaline HER

[21] 1.1. Autonomous System and Workflow

[22] Figure 2. Hardware composition of the platform. (A) Workflow of the automated platform: (1) instrument initialization and mass spectrometry (MS) calibration; (2) electrode acquisition from the electrode storage unit to retrieve a pristine electrode for catalyst preparation; (3) electrodeposition using a four-channel electrochemical cell for catalyst synthesis; (4) cleaning and drying of the electrochemical cell and electrodes using syringe and peristaltic pumps, followed by transferring to the drying station for subsequent electrolysis; (5) electrocatalysis in a dual-channel cell for H/D separation measurements (−50 mA cm−2) and electrochemical characterization, including LSV, CV, and EIS; (6) data analysis to evaluate overpotential (@ −10 mA cm−2, η10) and H/D separation coefficient (SH/D), with design of new experiments via Bayesian optimization. (B) Computer-aided design (CAD) of the automated platform with multiple modules.

[23] The system comprises six task-specific modules that work together to perform end-to-end preparation, online characterization, and real-time data processing for the catalysts (Figure 2 and SI Video 1). The catalyst preparation is through four-parallel electrodeposition cells with precise reagent dosing by syringe pumps and a 1 cm2 carbon-paper electrode from storage. The prepared co-deposited metal catalysts on the electrode were then collected by a robotic arm and moved to a washing station to remove the excess electrolyte, and later to a drying stack. The dried catalytic electrode was then sent to two of the H-cells for electrochemical analysis, as well as electrolysis and H/D separation factor (SH/D) quantification, respectively. The electrochemical tests, including linear scanning voltammetry (LSV), cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and galvanostatic electrolysis, were performed in 1.0 M KOH solution with 10% D2O. The SH/D was measured using a mass spectrometry (MS) analyzer. The generated H2/HD/D2 gas mixture on the headspace of the H-cells was collected by a syringe pump and then injected into the MS at a controlled speed. The MS was automatically calibrated at regular intervals using H2/HD/N2 gas. The system can analyze the MS data and the electrochemical measurement to extract overpotential and SH/D in real time, which is required in an autonomous closed-loop Bayesian optimization. A rail-mounted robotic arm coordinates material transfer across modules, enabling up to ~ 60 catalysts per day ( ≈ 20 min per catalyst) and ~ 5× higher throughput than manual operation.

[24] 2.  Machine Learning and Bayesian Optimization for the Catalysts

[25] 2.1. Metal-Dependent Descriptors for H/D Electrolytic Separation

[26] Using galvanostatic electrodeposition, a total of 561 unique catalyst formulations were evaluated in the initial screening, comprising 29 single-metal controls and 532 ordered bimetallic formulations. Sodium citrate (Na3Cit) was used as a coordination agent to regulate the deposition potential. For each bimetallic precursor solution, the two metals were denoted as “metal 1” and “metal 2” according to their relative precursor amounts, with “metal 1” being the major component. In the initial screening, the precursors of metal 1, metal 2, and Na3Cit were mixed at a molar ratio of 6:1:20/3, and electrodeposition was carried out at −300 mA cm−2 for 20 min. Electrolysis tests were performed in an H-type cell using 1.0 M KOH containing 10% D2O as the electrolyte, with a Pt wire as the counter electrode and Hg/HgO as the reference electrode. Catalysts containing Co or Fe as “element 1”, namely the major metal in the precursor formulation, were located close to the Pareto front for the dual-objective optimization (Figure 3A).

[27] To further probe the electrochemical origin of the H/D separation behavior, EIS spectra of the screened catalysts at the potential of −10 mA cm−2 were analyzed using the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) fitting45. Representative Rs-subtracted Nyquist plots and the corresponding DRT-fitted curves are shown in Figure 3B. Catalysts with high H/D separation coefficients, such as Co-Mo and Fe-Ni, generally exhibit compact impedance arcs, whereas low-separation catalysts show larger polarization features. This comparison indicates that enhanced H/D separation is associated with reduced interfacial relaxation resistance rather than simply with the uncompensated solution resistance.

[29] The DRT-derived parameters were further compared across the initial screening dataset (Figure 3C). The total DRT area, defined as the integrated relaxation contribution excluding Rs, shows a negative correlation with the H/D separation coefficient (Spearman ρ = −0.42, p = 2.25 × 10−25). Because Rs shows only a weak correlation with H/D separation, this trend indicates that the separation behavior is governed mainly by interfacial relaxation/polarization processes rather than by uncompensated solution resistance. Catalysts with higher H/D separation coefficients tend to cluster at lower total DRT area, suggesting that efficient interfacial charge transfer and reduced polarization favor isotope separation. This is consistent with a surface environment in which water dissociation, H*/D* formation, and subsequent hydrogen-isotope evolution proceed through a lower-resistance interfacial pathway. The DRT area at 0.8–20 ms also shows a weaker negative correlation with the H/D separation coefficient (Spearman ρ = −0.28, p = 3.28 × 10−11), although this trend is largely coupled to the total DRT area. This relaxation-time window may reflect kinetic contributions from intermediate-frequency interfacial processes, such as adsorbate formation/coverage relaxation and charge-transfer coupled surface reactions, but it should be interpreted as a secondary descriptor rather than an independent mechanistic origin.

[30] The DRT analysis served as an independent EIS-based validation that H/D separation is closely linked to interfacial relaxation and polarization processes. Guided by this electrochemical insight, machine learning was applied to identify the key factors governing H/D separation using a feature set comprising metal-related descriptors, adsorption-energy descriptors, and electrochemical parameters (see SI Section 3). The pipeline combined stratified train/test splitting via quantile binning, feature selection, ensemble-regression modeling, and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis to identify key drivers. A model with an R2 of 0.606 on the test set was obtained. Feature-importance ranking and analysis (Figure 3D) show that the key drivers are local adsorption and water-dissociation environment. A separate η10-oriented HER-activity analysis (SI Section 3) further showed that lower overpotentials are enriched in a high-work-function descriptor region, suggesting that activity and isotope separation are related but not governed by identical descriptor landscapes.

[31] Specifically, for H/D separation, descriptors associated with element 1 contribute strongly, especially electronegativity and H adsorption energy, indicating that isotope discrimination is primarily governed by the local adsorption-reaction environment associated with the major precursor element. Work-function of both elements, as a proxy for interfacial electron availability, also contributes strongly, suggesting that the electronic structure of the bimetallic system modulates electron-transfer coupling during O−H/O−D bond cleavage and H*/D* formation in the Volmer transition state. In comparison, descriptors such as M−M bonding, standard potential, H/D vibrational or ZPE terms, and OH adsorption energy provide secondary modulation, reflecting structural rigidity, redox tendency, isotope-sensitive bond stiffness, and water-dissociation-related effects. Together, these descriptors can modulate the zero-point energy difference between H- and D-containing intermediates and reshape the Volmer reaction barrier.

[32] Overall, H/D separation arises from isotope-dependent differences in the adsorption–reaction potential-energy surface at the active site, with electronegativity, H adsorption energetics, and work-function-related electronic structure describing the local environment that controls water dissociation and H*/D* evolution. The bimetallic architecture further tunes this landscape through electronic-structure shifts, M−M bonding, redox tendency, and isotope-sensitive vibrational or ZPE effects, ultimately modulating the extent of isotopic separation.

[33] 2.2. Bayesian Optimization towards Enhanced Catalytic Performance

[34] Four promising bimetallic systems were selected for Bayesian optimization (BO)46 to further enhance the H/D separation coefficient and HER activity. The search space was parameterized by the added volumes of two 0.15 M metal-salt stock solutions (1−19 mL each) and a 1.0 M sodium citrate solution (0−18 mL), with water added to maintain a total volume of 20 mL. A total of 28 experiments were performed iteratively in seven rounds, i.e., four experiments per round. The first two rounds were obtained by uniform sampling across the entire search space (Figure S10), while the subsequent five rounds were carried out using Bayesian optimization. The BO combines exploitation and exploration. Exploitation encourages regions predicted to have the highest values as the next to test, while exploration targets areas where the model has limited knowledge, effectively preventing the model from becoming trapped in local optima.

[36] The optimization was formulated as a dual-objective problem, targeting both a higher H/D separation coefficient (SH/D) at a fixed current of −50 mA cm−2 and a lower overpotential of HER at −10 mA cm−2 (η10). The reason we chose the different current density is to ensure accurate measurement of SH/D, which is more accurate at high current density due to the presence of a higher concentration of analytes, while the overpotential is more intrinsic to the catalysts at lower current density due to the absence of a mass-transport limit. A Gaussian process was selected as the surrogate model, and the qNoisy Expected Hypervolume Improvement (qNEHVI) algorithm47,48 was used to design each batch of four experiments, comprising three new reactions and one reference to validate system stability. The iterative procedure was carried out by the robotic platform until a predefined number of experiments had been completed.

[37] Across four representative bimetallic systems (CoMo, CoV, CoNi, and FeNi) (Figure 4A), the Pareto boundaries shift markedly outward after seven rounds, and the hypervolume improvement relative to batch 1 increases with iteration (Figure 4B). Quantitatively, the four selected systems showed high initial H/D separation coefficients in the primary screening, with SH/D = 9.8, 9.3, 8.5, and 9.0 for CoMo, CoV, CoNi, and FeNi, respectively. After seven BO batches, the best separation coefficients increased to 10.5, 9.6, 9.3, and 11.2 for CoMo, CoV, CoNi, and FeNi, respectively, while the lowest η10 values reached 73.0, 19.3, 85.3, and 128.8 mV. Because the maximum separation coefficient and the minimum overpotential are not always obtained from the same catalyst, the BO results are evaluated using Pareto-front expansion and hypervolume improvement relative to batch 1. Together, these results demonstrate that multi-objective BO efficiently improves the activity–selectivity landscape while providing a quantitative convergence signature through the evolution of hypervolume.

[38] To examine whether the BO search was consistent with the descriptor trends identified from the initial-screening dataset, we projected both the initial-screening samples and BO samples into weighted descriptor spaces (Figure 4C). The background descriptor maps were reconstructed from the initial-screening dataset only, whereas the BO samples were overlaid using metal-salt-ratio-weighted descriptors and colored by their measured H/D separation coefficients. High-separation BO samples cluster near the descriptor region enriched in high SH/D catalysts in the initial screening, characterized by intermediate H adsorption energy, moderate electronegativity, and a favorable work-function window. This agreement indicates that the descriptors selected by machine learning capture chemically meaningful trends for H/D separation, while BO further refines synthesis conditions within this favorable descriptor neighborhood to improve the activity-selectivity landscape. The location of this descriptor neighborhood is chemically consistent with alkaline HER: intermediate H adsorption reflects balanced H*/D* stabilization, while the work-function window suggests that interfacial electronic structure also modulates the electron-transfer component of O−H/O−D bond cleavage and H*/D* evolution. When considered together with the broader descriptor-space analysis on the initial-screening dataset, this local BO enrichment is consistent with a volcano-like separation landscape, in which high H/D separation is enriched within intermediate intermediate H adsorption-energy, electronegativity, and work-function windows rather than at the extremes of the descriptor space (SI Section 4).

[39] 3. Characterization of Catalysts

[40] Catalysts exhibiting superior separation performance (Table 1) were subjected to structural characterization. The CoMo, CoV, and CoNi catalysts all exhibit predominantly amorphous or poorly crystalline structures with uniformly distributed metal oxide species, as confirmed by SEM, TEM, XRD, and XPS analyses (Figure 5 and SI Section 5). In contrast, the FeNi catalyst shows a crystalline alloy-dominated structure with minor surface oxidation. Morphologically, CoMo and FeNi display flower-like architectures (Figure 5A, D), CoV presents stacked platelets (Figure 5B), and CoNi shows a walnut-like structure (Figure 5C). Overall, these results indicate that the catalysts differ mainly in their crystallinity and composition, while maintaining homogeneous elemental distributions (Figure 5A-D).

[47] For most catalysts, the H/D separation coefficient in HER decreases with increasing temperature and cathodic current density, indicating that faster H2 evolution generally weakens isotope discrimination. We can fit the temperature-dependent separation coefficient to an Arrhenius equation to obtain the difference in activation energy of H vs. D in the rate-determining step (ΔEa). The negative ΔEa values obtained for Pt sheet (ΔEa = −6.5 kJ/mol), Co (ΔEa = −6.5 kJ/mol), CoV (ΔEa = −9.6 kJ/mol), CoMo (ΔEa = −4.6 kJ/mol), and FeNi (ΔEa = −6.4 kJ/mol) at −50 mA cm−2 are consistent with a kinetically favored H-involving pathway relative to the corresponding D-involving process (SI Section 7). Similarly, the lower separation coefficients observed at higher current density suggest partial suppression of the apparent isotope effect under stronger polarization, likely reflecting changes in the interfacial reaction environment, including variations in overpotential, surface H coverage, local pH, and bubble perturbation.

[48] As shown in Figure 6A-B, CoV and CoMo exhibit the highest separation coefficients under mild conditions but show more pronounced decay under higher current density, whereas FeNi retains relatively high values at high current density. Notably, CoMo catalyst appears to undergo alkali- and bias-induced surface reconstruction under alkaline HER conditions. In situ Raman showed the disappearance of the Mo–O band in alkaline electrolyte and the emergence of Co(OH)2 features with increasing cathodic polarization (Figure S19). XRD further confirmed the presence of Co(OH)2 in the catalyst after HER (Figure S16). This interpretation is consistent with recent studies49,50 showing that Co-Mo precatalysts reconstruct into Co(OH)2-rich interfaces under alkaline HER conditions, which promote water dissociation, a proton/deuteron-transfer-sensitive step.

[49] 4.2. FeNi Catalyst Stability and Deuterium Enrichment from Water

[50] Based on the separation performance under varying temperature and current density (Figure 6A-B), FeNi was selected for further stability tests and deuterium enrichment. The long-term stability of the FeNi catalyst was evaluated in electrolyzers with active areas of 1, 4, and 16 cm2 using 1.0 M KOH as the electrolyte. A 200-hour stability test (Figure 6C) was first performed in an H-type electrolyzer at room temperature at −50 mA cm−2, during which the separation coefficient was measured by mass spectrometry every 10 hours. Over continuous electrolysis, the working potential shifted only slightly from −0.250 to −0.275 V, corresponding to an overpotential increase of ~25 mV, while the H/D separation coefficient decreased by only ~0.2. These results indicate that FeNi largely maintained both catalytic activity and isotope separation performance during prolonged operation.

[51] Subsequently, the FeNi catalyst was assembled into a commercially available anion exchange membrane (AEM) electrolyzer with an electrode area of 2 × 2 cm2 to preliminarily evaluate its stability under more practically relevant conditions (Figure 6D). The test was carried out in 1.0 M KOH at −500 mA cm−2 and 80 °C, with a commercially available Ni3Fe serving as the anode catalyst. Under these conditions, the FeNi catalyst exhibited good operational stability over 300 hours of continuous electrolysis, with the overall water-splitting voltage remaining stable at approximately 1.88 V. These results further demonstrated the promise of FeNi for scaled water electrolysis under practically relevant conditions.

[52] Based on the above results on the temperature- and current-density-dependent separation coefficients, together with the stability evaluation, the FeNi catalyst was ultimately selected and assembled into an AEM electrolyzer with an electrode area of 16 cm2 for multistage deuterium enrichment from water. The enrichment stage was carried out in 1.0 M KOH at room temperature under a current density of −500 mA cm−2. From stage to stage, the enriched water was evaporated from the KOH solution after electrolysis, and the quantities of D were characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The concentration increased steadily from the initial 0.0156% to 0.09%, 0.45%, 2.33%, 12.72%, 44.95%, and eventually 98.75% over six stages, demonstrating the practical usage of the catalyst and multistaged enrichment strategy for D enrichment (Figure 6E and SI Section 7).

[53] Conclusion

[54] In summary, we present an integrated platform that advances electrocatalytic H/D separation as a practical complement to green hydrogen production. By coupling end-to-end automation—catalyst synthesis, electrochemical evaluation, and online isotope quantification—with real-time data acquisition and analysis, the system enables rapid, reproducible discovery of catalysts that simultaneously target isotope selectivity and hydrogen-evolution performance.

[55] From the collected large-scale dataset, interpretable machine learning resolves a compact descriptor map in which electronegativity, H adsorption energetics, and work-function govern H/D separation, with M–M bonding, standard potential, and isotope vibrational/ZPE descriptors acting as secondary descriptors. Guided by this map, multi-objective Bayesian optimization autonomously expands the activity-selectivity Pareto front, and its sampling trajectory follows the same physically meaningful descriptor trends, indicating feedback-driven, interpretable convergence rather than unconstrained black-box search.

[56] Long-term galvanostatic operation further confirms that selected catalysts retain both separation performance and activity. More broadly, this work demonstrates a general strategy for data-centric, autonomous catalyst discovery that can accelerate the development of electrochemical technologies critical to a low-carbon energy future.

[60] Robotic Platforms

[61] The platform consists of six functional modules enabling automated catalyst synthesis via electrodeposition and subsequent performance evaluation. These modules include an electrode storage module, a material transfer module, an electrodeposition module for catalyst fabrication, a catalyst post-treatment module, an electrochemical testing module, and a mass spectrometry module. Together, these components form an integrated electrochemical system that enables streamlined catalyst preparation, post-treatment, and characterization. Detailed descriptions of each module are provided in SI Section 1.

[62] Calibration of H2 and HD in Mass Spectroscopy

[63] The mass spectrometry testing module is shown in Figure S2. A mass flow controller was used to precisely regulate the N2 carrier gas flow at 20 mL min−1, and a high-precision syringe pump was employed for controlled gas sampling to ensure stable and reproducible data acquisition. The mass spectrometer was calibrated after each instrument restart using standard gas mixtures with known concentrations (N2, HD, and H2). The calibration curves for H2 and HD were established by correlating signal intensities with gas compositions, showing excellent linearity (R2 > 0.99). Detailed experimental procedures are provided in SI Section 1.

[64] Catalyst Preparation

[65] Preparation of Screening Catalysts: The catalysts for preliminary screening were prepared by galvanostatic electrodeposition. Metal precursor stock solutions (0.15 M) and sodium citrate (Na3Cit, 1.0 M) were prepared in advance. The electrodeposition bath was formulated by mixing metal precursor 1, metal precursor 2, and Na3Cit in a volume ratio of 6:1:1 and diluting to a total volume of 20 mL with deionized water, followed by thorough homogenization. The catalysts were then electrodeposited onto the working electrode (carbon paper, 1 cm2) at a constant current density of −300 mA cm−2 for 20 min. After deposition, the electrodes were rinsed with deionized water and dried for 2 min under far-infrared lamps. Detailed catalyst compositions and additional experimental procedures are provided in SI Section 2.

[66] Preparation of FeNi Catalysts: For the preparation of FeNi catalysts for long-term electrolysis, the electrodeposition electrolyte was prepared using 0.25 M Fe2(SO4)3, 0.50 M NiSO4, 0.25 M H3BO3, 0.50 M NaCl, and 1.16 M Na3Cit. The catalysts were fabricated by galvanostatic electrodeposition on carbon paper (25 cm2) at a constant current of 2.5 A for 20 min. After electrodeposition, the obtained electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual electrolyte and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 8 h. FeNi electrodes with geometric areas of 4 cm2 and 16 cm2 were subsequently obtained by cutting from a larger 25 cm2 electrode prepared under identical conditions. Detailed information on the synthesis and characterization of the FeNi catalysts is provided in SI Section 7.

[67] DRT-Fitting of the EIS

[68] EIS data from the initial screening dataset were analyzed by distribution of relaxation times (DRT) fitting using pyDRTtools (https://github.com/ciuccislab/pyDRTtools). The real and imaginary impedance components were used to recover the relaxation-time distribution, with the solution resistance (Rs) separated from the relaxation contribution. The total DRT area, defined as the integrated relaxation contribution excluding Rs, was extracted as a measure of the overall interfacial relaxation/polarization resistance. A tau-resolved DRT area in the 0.8-20 ms window was also calculated. These DRT-derived quantities were used for electrochemical interpretation only and were not included in the machine-learning descriptor set. Further details are provided in SI Section 3 and https://github.com/Wang-Group/HD_separation.

[69] Machine Learning Algorithms

[70] Machine-learning models were constructed using the initial-screening dataset of 561 catalysts. For H/D separation, the prediction target was the H/D separation coefficient measured at −50 mA cm−2. The candidate descriptor pool included an LSV-derived signed current descriptor, Rs estimated from the high-frequency real impedance in EIS spectra, and element-level physicochemical descriptors for metal 1 and metal 2. The dataset was split into training and test sets using an 80/20 split with quantile-based stratification of the target distribution. Feature selection was performed in two stages: high-separation classification (>8) was first used to identify a compact set of core descriptors, and Random-Forest/Extra-Trees regression with forward feature addition was then used to select the final 10-feature descriptor set. The final model was interpreted using SHAP analysis. A separate activity-oriented machine-learning analysis was also performed using the overpotential at −10 mA cm−2 (η10) as the prediction target, excluding direct current-proxy descriptors to avoid leakage. Detailed information on the machine-learning methods and results is provided in SI Section 3 and https://github.com/Wang-Group/HD_separation.

[71] Bayesian Optimization

[72] Bayesian optimization (BO) was implemented using the BoTorch framework with heteroskedastic Gaussian process (GP) surrogate models trained on the experimental dataset. Two objectives were jointly optimized: the overpotential at −10 mA cm−2 and the H/D separation coefficient, representing catalytic activity and isotopic selectivity, respectively. A Matérn kernel with automatic relevance determination (ARD) was employed, and model hyperparameters were optimized via marginal log-likelihood. Multi-objective optimization was performed using the q-Noisy Expected Hypervolume Improvement (qNEHVI) acquisition function. At each iteration, candidate conditions were selected by maximizing the acquisition function, and experiments were conducted in batches to efficiently balance exploration and exploitation of the design space. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in SI Section 4.

[73] Characterizations

[74] Detailed experimental procedures for electrochemistry and structural characterization of the catalysts are provided in the SI Sections 5 and 6.

[75] Electrolysis of Water

[76] H-type electrolytic cell experiments: The long-term stability of the FeNi catalyst was evaluated under continuous electrolysis at a current density of −50 mA cm−2 for 200 h. Gas samples collected from the headspace of the electrochemical cell were analyzed by mass spectrometry at intervals of approximately 10 h to monitor both catalytic activity and H/D separation performance over time. The catalyst had a geometric area of 1 × 1 cm2, and the electrolyte was 1.0 M KOH containing 10% D2O. All measurements were conducted at ambient temperature.

[77] 2.0 × 2.0 cm2 AEMWE Experiments: Water electrolysis tests were conducted in a single-cell anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE, KMem AE01, Xiamen Kah Membrane Technology Ltd.) with a working area of 2.0 × 2.0 cm2. The membrane electrode assembly was assembled using nickel-plated bipolar plates and PTFE gaskets. Before testing, the cell was conditioned in 1.0 M KOH at 80 °C for 4 h. Electrochemical measurements were performed using a battery tester (CT5001A, Wuhan LAND Electronic Co., Ltd.) at 80 °C with an electrolyte flow rate of 5.0 mL min−1. Long-term durability was evaluated galvanostatically at 0.5 A cm−2.

[78] 16 cm2 AEMWE Experiments: Water electrolysis tests were conducted in a single-cell anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE, Nanjing Daquan Zhongke Hydrogen Energy Technology Co., Ltd.) with a 16 cm2 electrode area. A DQ-AEM080 membrane (80 μm) was used, and the cell was activated by circulating 1.0 M KOH at 80 °C for 4 h before measurements. Electrolyte (1.0 M KOH) was continuously fed at 90 mL min−1. For deuterium enrichment experiments, the FeNi catalyst was employed in a 16 cm2 AEMWE under room temperature operation at a constant current density of −500 mA cm−2. A multistage enrichment strategy was implemented, in which the electrolyte for each subsequent stage was freshly prepared by adjusting the deuterium concentration according to quantitative 2H NMR analysis of the previous stage, rather than directly reusing the collected electrolyte. Detailed experimental procedures and results are provided in SI Section 7.

[79] Quantification of D in Water

[80] The deuterium (D) content in the electrolyte was quantified by 2H nuclear magnetic resonance (2H NMR) spectroscopy using an internal standard method. After electrolysis, a known volume of the collected electrolyte was mixed with a DMSO-d6 solution of known concentration. 2H NMR spectra were recorded on an Avance III HD 850 MHz spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin GmbH, Germany) at ambient temperature. Quantitative analysis was performed by comparing the integrated peak area of HDO with that of the DMSO-d6 internal standard, and the D concentration was calculated based on the ratio of the corresponding peak integrals. A total of 48 scans were acquired for each spectrum to ensure an adequate signal-to-noise ratio. Detailed results and corresponding 2H NMR spectra are provided in SI Section 7.
